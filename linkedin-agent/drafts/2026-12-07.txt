Good SOPs describe the process. Great SOPs make the wrong step hard to take.

Too many SOPs are written for the auditor, not the operator. They're complete, they're approved, and nobody on the floor opens them.

What changes that:
- One action per step. If a step has "and" in it, it's probably two steps.
- Steps in the order the work is done, not the order the equipment manual lists them.
- Limits and acceptance criteria inside the step, not in an annexure three pages later.
- The same words the operators use for the rooms and the equipment.
- Walk the draft on the floor with the person who does the job before it goes for approval.

That last one catches more problems than any review cycle.

If an operator needs a training session to understand an SOP, it's the SOP that needs rewriting.
